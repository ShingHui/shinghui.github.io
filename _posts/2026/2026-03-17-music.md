  <!-- ══ MOTIVATION ══ -->
  <div class="sn-section-tag">01 &nbsp; Motivation</div>
  <div class="sn-section-title">Is ambient sound a cognitive catalyst?</div>
  <div class="sn-motivation">
    In behavioural intervention and everyday study, we often try to improve focus by modifying the environment — for example, by playing background music. But does music <strong>tempo</strong> actually enhance <strong>reading comprehension performance</strong>?
    <br><br>
    This experiment was born from a simple observation within our group: all of us study with different kinds of music. We designed a controlled quantitative study using <strong>G*Power</strong> for sample size estimation and <strong>R Studio (one-way ANOVA)</strong> for analysis — and ultimately confronted a null result that taught us more than a significant one ever could.
  </div>

      <div class="sn-rca-left">
        <div class="sn-rca-num">CONFOUND · 01</div>
        <div class="sn-rca-title">Individual Baseline Capability</div>
        <span class="sn-rca-badge badge-red">High Impact</span>
      </div>
      <div class="sn-rca-right">
        <div class="sn-rca-bug">
          <span class="tag-bug">Bug</span>
          Each participant's pre-existing verbal ability varied widely, diluting the small effect music tempo may have produced. We had no pre-test to account for this.
        </div>
        <div class="sn-rca-fix">
          <span class="tag-fix">Next Step</span>
          Add a <strong>pre-test (baseline measurement)</strong> and apply <strong>ANCOVA</strong> to statistically control for individual ability — directly mirroring best practice in behavioural intervention design.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="sn-rca-card">
      <div class="sn-rca-left">
        <div class="sn-rca-num">CONFOUND · 02</div>
        <div class="sn-rca-title">Environmental Noise Contamination</div>
        <span class="sn-rca-badge badge-amber">Medium Impact</span>
      </div>
      <div class="sn-rca-right">
        <div class="sn-rca-bug">
          <span class="tag-bug">Bug</span>
          Testing in a shared computer lab meant mouse-click sounds and volume variation from other participants became uncontrolled auditory distractors — especially damaging for the silence condition. Post-test questionnaires confirmed multiple participants flagged this.
        </div>
        <div class="sn-rca-fix">
          <span class="tag-fix">Next Step</span>
          Use isolated booths, require <strong>noise-cancelling headphones</strong> across all conditions, and record ambient noise levels in dB as a covariate.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="sn-rca-card">
      <div class="sn-rca-left">
        <div class="sn-rca-num">CONFOUND · 03</div>
        <div class="sn-rca-title">Protocol Adherence & UX Failure</div>
        <span class="sn-rca-badge badge-blue">Design Issue</span>
      </div>
      <div class="sn-rca-right">
        <div class="sn-rca-bug">
          <span class="tag-bug">Bug</span>
          Two participants began answering before instructions were complete, corrupting reaction time data at the source.
        </div>
        <div class="sn-rca-fix">
          <span class="tag-fix">Next Step</span>
          <strong>Lock the response button</strong> until instructions finish — a simple UX constraint that enforces protocol adherence systemically, removing reliance on participant compliance.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="sn-rca-card">
      <div class="sn-rca-left">
        <div class="sn-rca-num">CONFOUND · 04</div>
        <div class="sn-rca-title">Demand Characteristics & Music Familiarity</div>
        <span class="sn-rca-badge badge-amber">Often Overlooked</span>
      </div>
      <div class="sn-rca-right">
        <div class="sn-rca-bug">
          <span class="tag-bug">Bug</span>
          The recruitment form directly stated the study topic ("Effects of Music on Learning"), potentially priming participants' expectations before they arrived. Additionally, the specific songs used — piano covers of popular Taiwanese pop songs — were recognisable to many participants, introducing familiarity as an uncontrolled variable.
        </div>
        <div class="sn-rca-fix">
          <span class="tag-fix">Next Step</span>
          Use a <strong>masked recruitment title</strong> to prevent demand characteristics. Conduct a <strong>familiarity pre-screen</strong> and select stimuli participants have not heard before, or explicitly measure and control for familiarity as a covariate.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

  <!-- ══ CLINICAL APPLICATION ══ -->
  <div class="sn-section-tag" style="margin-top:36px;">07 &nbsp; Clinical Relevance</div>
  <div class="sn-section-title">Why this matters beyond the lab</div>
  <div class="sn-motivation" style="border-left-color: var(--sn-blue); background: rgba(88,166,255,0.05);">
    Even a null result carries practical value. If no specific tempo reliably boosts performance, it suggests that <strong>personal familiarity and habit</strong> — not a universal "optimal" music condition — are the real drivers of cognitive efficiency. This reframes the question for applied settings:<br><br>
    In <strong>clinical and therapeutic environments</strong> (waiting rooms, hospital wards, counselling spaces), background music selection should be individualised. Research shows instrumental music can reduce depression symptoms and anxiety, and improve cognitive performance in patients (Aalbers, 2017; Mina, 2021). Rather than prescribing a single soundtrack, practitioners should assess each patient's habitual sound environment and match interventions accordingly — the same principle that underpins person-centred psychological care.
  </div>

More importantly, the reversed result — slow tempo outperforming fast — pushed us to question not just the data, but the theory behind the hypothesis.
